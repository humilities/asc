                                   1. Supercomputing Activities
   Xiangtan University(XTU) is a prestigious comprehensive national key university and a “Dou-
ble First-Class” construction university of the country. Standing majestically in the hometown of
the great leader Mao Zedong, XTU is a place of great renown. XTU not only has modern teaching
facilities, but also is equipped with an advanced high-performance computing center. In terms of
high-performance talent cultivation, the school provides students with abundant high-performance
learning resources, including cutting-edge professional courses, high-level academic lectures, inno-
vative research projects, and a powerful high-performance computing platforms. These resources
not only broaden students’ horizons, but also provide them solid academic and technical support.
Last year, we participated in the ASC competition for the first time. Although we didn’t make
it to the finals, our ranking of 46th was very encouraging for the students, and the school also
gave us high recognition. Next, we will introduce the university’s supercomputing platform and
supercomputing-related activities.
1.1. The HPC Platform of XTU.
   Xiangtan University’s Super Heterogeneous Parallel Computing Cluster is a key project sup-
ported by the provincial finance under the “4th Five-Year Plan” for new infrastructure construction.
The cluster includes ClusterEngine high-performance computing platform and AIstation artificial
intelligence platform, and is open to all teachers and students of the university. The computing
capacity of the cluster is the theoretical computing capacity of 509 trillion times of CPU double-
precision peak performance (6,112 physical cores) and 1552 trillion times of GPU single-precision
theoretical computing capacity. The overall logical framework design diagram of the cluster is
shown in the figure.
   The ClusterEngine high-performance computing platform consists of 1 management node, 1
login node, 1 physical monitoring platform, 85 general-purpose computing nodes, 2 large-memory
computing nodes, totaling 5472 CPU cores and a total memory capacity of 44.5TB. The nodes
are interconnected through Gigabit Ethernet and Infiniband. The management and login nodes are
each equipped with 16 CPU cores and 64GB of memory.
   The cluster has two resource partitions: CU and FAT partitions. The CU partition is configured
with 80 general-purpose computing nodes, each with 64 Intel 8358 CPU cores and 512GB of mem-
ory, totaling 5,120 CPU cores. The FAT partition is configured with 2 large-memory computing
nodes, each with 16 Intel 6334 CPU cores and 1TB of memory.
   The main function of the platform is to provide high-performance scientific and engineering com-
puting services to the entire university, meeting the needs of various disciplines for large-scale data
processing and large-scale scientific computing. The platform’s main application areas include pro-
viding high-performance computing environments for mathematics, statistics, mechanics, physics,
chemistry, chemical engineering, biology, earth science, aerospace science and technology, com-
puter science, and many other disciplines. The platform supports a wide range of application soft-
ware, such as MATLAB, OpenFOAM, ABINIT,GROMACS, NAMD and so on.
   The AIstation artificial intelligence platform consists of 10 GPU computing nodes, with a total
of 640 CPU cores, 80 NVIDIA A100 acceleration cards, a total memory capacity of 5TB, and
shared with the parallel storage and high-performance computing platform. The total capacity
is 1.3PB. The GPU’s measured peak speed can reach 888.9 trillion operations per second. This
platform supports single-machine and distributed running of mainstream AI frameworks as well as
task visualization.

1.4. Research and Applications.
   The research and application achievements of XTU in the field of supercomputing are remark-
able, including parallel solvers, the development of high performance computing software, the op-
timization of computer system architecture, the design of high performance computing algorithms
and the application of high performance computing in scientific research, engineering design, in-
dustrial production and other fields. The following is only a brief list.
   For example, the JXPAMG solver jointly developed with the Beijing Institute of Applied Physics
and Computational Mathematics has become the default linear solver of JASMIN, a high-performance
parallel computing software platform, with its algorithm innovation, efficient parallel performance
and multi-field applicability, and its comprehensive performance is better than the international
popular similar solvers. The solver has greatly improved the computational efficiency of some
defense application model problems, and has an important influence in the field of scientific cal-
culation and engineering simulation.
   The chemical flooding model is widely used in the numerical simulation of complex reservoir
because of its high oil recovery. Therefore, for the numerical simulation of chemical flooding reser-
voir, CPU-GPU heterogeneous parallel discretization technology is used to discretized the chemical

1.5. Key Achievements.
   As mentioned above, XTU’s research in supercomputing includes many aspects, and have pub-
lished multiple papers in international journals and conferences in the fields of mathematics and
computer science and so on. Such as CCF Transactions on High Performance Computing, Appli-
cable Analysis, Mathematical Methods in the Applied Sciences, International Conference on Man-
agement of Data(SIGMOD) International Conference on Very Large Data Bases(VLDB), IEEE
Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems(TPDS) and other journal conferences, here is a
brief overview of two of the outcomes:
   Innovation 1: Parallel Reservoir Simulators on Tianhe-2 [1].
   Parallel reservoir simulators are now widely used with availability of super computers. Modern
massively parallel supercomputers demonstrate great power for simulating large-scale reservoir
models. However, improving scalability and efficiency for fully implicit methods on emerging
parallel architectures is still challenging. In this work, we present a robust discretization together
with a parallel linear solver algorithm; and we explore the parallel implementation on the world’s
fastest supercomputer Tianhe-2. Starting with a general compositional model, we focus on the black
oil model and developed Parallel eXtension Framework for parallelizing the serial simulator. A
parallel preconditioner based on fast auxiliary space preconditioning (FASP) is applied to solve the
Jacobian system arising from the fully implicit discretization. The parallel simulator was validated
using large-scale black oil benchmark problems, for which parallel scalabilities were tested. Giant
reservoir models with over 100 million grid blocks have been simulated within a few minutes,
and test the strong scalability of AMG solver with 1 billion unknown. We also demonstrate the
parallelization and acceleration using Intel Xeon Phi coprocessors. In the end, the efficiency of the
parallel simulator is illustrated by a giant reservoir using up to 10,000 cores, for which the CPU
and communication time are summarized for the linear and nonlinear algorithms.
   Innovation 2: Efficient Algorithms for the Maxwell Eigenvalue Problem [2]. The Maxwell
eigenvalue problem is a basic and important computational model in computational electromag-
netism, e.g., in regard to electromagnetic waveguides and resonances in cavities. This work pro-
posed two two-grid methods for the Maxwell eigenvalue problem. The new two-grid schemes re-
duce the solution of the Maxwell eigenvalue problem on a fine grid to one linear indefinite Maxwell
equation on the same fine grid and an original eigenvalue problem on a much coarser grid. The
new schemes, therefore, save total computational cost. The error estimates reveals that the two-
grid methods maintain asymptotically optimal accuracy, and the numerical experiments presented
confirm the theoretical results.

4.2. Performance Optimization Methods.
4.2.1. Modification of “HPL.dat”.
   In the data document HPL.dat of HPL, many parameters affect the test results of HPL, among
which the most important parameters are the problem size N , the block size N B, and the process
array configuration (P × Q). Their optimal values are usually determined by factors such as the
structure of the CPU, and we can obtain their optimal values for HPL through multiple estimates
                                                                                                    16

and tests. The other parameters have a smaller impact on the results, and we can also determine
their optimal values through partial fine-tuning.
    (1) The size of the matrix N s: The N value determines the size of the matrix. And the
        larger the scale N of the matrix, the higher the proportion of effective computation and the
        higher floating-point processing performance of the system. However, at the same time,
        the increase in matrix scale N will lead to an increase in memory consumption. Once the
        actual memory space of the system is insufficient, using the cache will significantly reduce
        performance. Therefore, for general systems, it is necessary to try to increase the scale N of
        the matrix while ensuring that the system cache is not used. Considering that the operating
        system itself requires a certain amount of memory, in addition to matrix A (N × N ), HPL
        also has other memory overheads, and communication also needs to occupy some cache (the
        specific size of which varies depending on different MPIs). Generally, matrix A occupying
        about 80% of the total system memory is optimal N × N × 8=total system memory 80%.
    (2) The size of the matrix block(N Bs): N B represents the size of matrix block division.
        The size of the calculation granularity N B affects the pros and cons of the calculation
        performance heavily. The coefficient matrix is divided into cyclic blocks of N B × N B and
        assigned to each process Medium to medium processing, too coarse or too fine particles will
        affect performance. Because the communication between cluster nodes adopts the message
        passing method, the N B is too small to pass messages frequently between nodes, resulting
        in long communication time, short computing time, and low computing/communication
        performance ratio; The N B is too large, which can easily lead to uneven load, which will
        also increase communication overhead. Therefore, communication between and within
        nodes also requires trade-offs. The selection of N B value is mainly to obtain the optimal
        value through actual testing. According to the test, N B should not be too large or too small,
        generally below 512, generally a multiple of 64.
    (3) Two-dimensional processor grid (P × Q): P × Q represents the process grid, which is
        equivalent to the number of processors in the cluster. When choosing these two numbers,
        try to make the grid as “square-shape” as possible in shape. As stated on the HPL website,
        the best practice is to make the grid as close to a “squar” as possible, that is, P and Q
        should be approximately equal and Q should be slightly larger than P . Therefore, the two-
        dimensional processor grid (P × Q) should best meet the following requirements:
           P × Q= the number of processes;
           P × Q= the number of GPUs = the number of processes.
           One process corresponds to one GPU, and a process can spawn multiple threads to satu-
        rate the CPU.
           P ≤ Q.
           Through multiple tests, the program is run with the number of threads=16, and it is found
        that when the values of P and Q are 4, 4 or 2, 8, the running results show better.

5.1. Analysis of HPCG.
   The High Performance Conjugate Gradient (HPCG) benchmark is a key tool for assessing the
performance of high-performance computing (HPC) systems, particularly in simulating real-world
computational workloads. Unlike traditional benchmarks such as HPL, which mainly measure
floating-point operations through dense matrix multiplication, HPCG focuses on solving large
sparse linear systems. These systems are derived from the discretization of a three-dimensional par-
tial differential equation (PDE) that simulates a heat diffusion problem with zero Dirichlet boundary
conditions.
   At the heart of HPCG is the preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method, which neces-
sitates efficient handling of sparse matrix operations, optimized memory bandwidth, and parallel
computation across distributed systems. The global problem is defined on a three-dimensional grid
with dimensions (nx × npx) × (ny × npy) × (nz × npz), where nx, ny, and nz represent the local
subgrid sizes in the x, y, and z dimensions, respectively, assigned to each MPI process. The values
npx, npy, and npz denote the number of processes in each corresponding dimension. These values
are automatically computed during the HPCG setup phase but can also be manually specified in
the input file “hpcg.dat” or provided as command-line arguments.
   The HPCG benchmark consists of three critical computational kernels, each integral to evaluating
modern supercomputer architectures:
     (1) Sparse Matrix-Vector Multiplication (SpMV): The core computational task of HPCG, SpMV
         is computationally intensive and central to the benchmark’s performance evaluation.
     (2) Symmetric Gauss-Seidel (SymGS) Relaxation: This serves as the essential precondition-
         ing technique for the conjugate gradient method, enhancing the efficiency of the iterative
         solution process.
     (3) Conjugate Gradient Method: The iterative approach used to solve the large-scale sparse
         linear systems, making it a crucial component of HPCG’s overall performance.
   These kernels are commonly found in scientific computing applications, such as computational
fluid dynamics and electromagnetic simulations. They present significant challenges for system
bandwidth and fine-grained parallel efficiency, making them indispensable for benchmarking mod-
ern HPC architectures.
5.2. Performance Optimization Methods.
5.2.1. Modification of “HPCG.dat”.
  By modifying the configuration parameters in the “HPCG.dat” file, the optimization of the stan-
dard HPCG benchmark can be significantly impacted. The file contains two key sections that can be
adjusted to improve performance: the problem size (specifically, the matrix size) and the runtime.
Adjusting the matrix size can have a major effect on performance.
    (1) Matrix Size: The matrix size defines the scale of the problem and is directly related to the
        global dimensions of the discretized computation grid. By adjusting the local subgrid di-
        mensions (nx, ny, nz) and the number of subgrids in each dimension (npx, npy, npz), the
        workload assigned to each MPI process can be optimized. A larger matrix size generally
        increases computational complexity, which improves the scalability and performance of
                                                                                                 22

       the benchmark on a large number of processors. However, excessively large problem sizes
       may lead to memory limitations or imbalanced workload distribution between processes.
       Therefore, careful consideration of these parameters is essential for achieving optimal per-
       formance.
   (2) Runtime: The runtime is another important parameter that influences the performance of
       the HPCG benchmark. A longer runtime allows for more thorough testing of the system’s
       ability to handle sustained computational load. While a longer runtime provides a better
       measure of the system’s long-term stability and computing capacity, excessively long run-
       times may lead to diminishing returns in performance due to hardware bottlenecks. There-
       fore, choosing an appropriate runtime is essential to strike a balance between accuracy and
       benchmark duration. In addition to the size of the topic scale, the shortest running time
       should be set to ensure the long enough running time of the system. Optimizing the matrix
       size and runtime in the “HPCG.dat” file is crucial for fine-tuning benchmark performance,
       ensuring that the results reflect the system’s true capabilities. These modifications help

   In a dual-socket NUMA configuration, CPUs, memory, and GPUs are physically partitioned
across distinct hardware nodes (sockets). Consequently, when a computational process residing on
one node attempts to access memory or GPU resources located on the other, the data must traverse
the interconnect bus (e.g., QuickPath Interconnect or UltraPath Interconnect). This cross-node
communication introduces substantial latency and is strictly constrained by the bandwidth limits
of the bus. During intensive, large-scale inference tasks, such architectural constraints frequently
induce data congestion, manifesting as a critical performance bottleneck.
   To mitigate this bottleneck, we utilize the numactl utility to enforce strict hardware locality. By
leveraging OS-level scheduling, this approach confines script execution, CPU scheduling, memory
allocation, and target GPU utilization entirely within a single physical node. This strategy guar-
antees a precise topological alignment between computational tasks and their requisite hardware
resources. By circumventing the redundant overhead associated with cross-node communication,
this method substantially enhances the overall system throughput and accelerates model inference,
resulting in an approximately 23.4% reduction in inference time as shown in Table 9(the
baseline test on “Server B” is shown in Table 18). Since NUMA binding operates strictly as
an OS-level optimization for hardware resource scheduling, it does not interfere with the model’s
underlying mathematical logic. Consequently, the PSNR of the generated videos remains un-
affected, yielding a mean variance of zero.
6.2.2. Mixed Precision.
   The fundamental principle of mixed-precision optimization relies on the fact that deep neural
networks are generally insensitive to minor numerical variances, allowing them to maintain output
accuracy without requiring extremely high decimal precision. During model inference, replacing
standard 32-bit floating-point (FP32) formats with 16-bit BF16 or TF32 (TensorFloat-32) formats
directly halves the total data volume of model parameters and intermediate computations. This
reduction significantly alleviates the data transfer payload between system memory and the GPU,
effectively mitigating I/O bottlenecks and accelerating transmission speeds. Specifically, BF16 re-
tains the same exponent width as FP32 by truncating the mantissa, thereby preserving the dynamic
range; meanwhile, TF32 leverages hardware-level advantages for low bit-width multiplication dur-
ing internal computations. Furthermore, because the individual data elements are shorter, modern
GPU computing units can process a greater number of floating-point operations in parallel within
a single clock cycle, multiplicatively increasing the throughput of existing hardware. In summary,
mixed precision trades unnecessary decimal precision for a smaller memory footprint and faster
data flow, tangibly improving overall inference speed while ensuring minimal degradation in the
generated video quality (PSNR).
   Regarding specific implementation, this optimization can be divided into three independent op-
tional components.
    (1) First, at the global backend configuration level, we enable hardware-level TF32 accelera-
        tion torch.backends.cuda.matmul.allow_tf32 = True and torch.backends.cudnn.allow_tf32
        = True. These directives allow the CUDA and cuDNN core libraries to automatically con-
        vert input FP32 data into the TF32 format during matrix multiplications and convolutions.
        The TF32 format utilizes an 8-bit exponent to maintain the same dynamic range as FP32
        to prevent overflow, while retaining a 10-bit mantissa to satisfy the model’s precision re-
        quirements.
    (2) Second, we introduce a BF16-based Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) inference architec-
        ture within the core diffusion denoising loops, such as action sequence vector generation
        and video sequence frame generation. By utilizing the torch.autocast(device_type“cuda”,

6.2.3. I/O Improvements.
   We employed three distinct I/O optimization methods, which are described as follows.
   (1) Mmap-Based Loading Optimization for Massive Model Weights
   In the I/O optimization phase, we systematically refactored the loading mechanism for the mas-
sive 16.7 GB model checkpoint (unifolm_wma_dual.ckpt). Specifically, we enabled memory map-
ping by setting mmap=True within the torch.load function, fundamentally streamlining the un-
derlying data flow.
   Under the default loading paradigm, data must traverse a strictly serial path: SSD → OS Kernel
Buffer → User Space Memory → GPU VRAM(Video Random Access Memory). By implement-
ing memory mapping, the file data is loaded exclusively into the kernel buffer. PyTorch can then
access this data directly, circumventing redundant user-space copying and eliminating the sub-
stantial latency overhead associated with duplicating approximately 16 GB of data within the host
memory.
   Crucially, the mmap mechanism facilitates asynchronous, on-demand data retrieval. When
executing subsequent operations to physically migrate the weights to the GPU (e.g., invoking
model.cuda()), the system triggers page faults to fetch the actual data from the SSD in standard
page increments (typically 4KB or 2MB).
                                                                                                  32

        This behavior allows the PCIe bus to perform concurrent data transfers—moving data from the
     SSD to the kernel buffer while simultaneously transferring loaded pages from host memory to the
     GPU VRAM—thereby establishing a highly efficient pipelined I/O architecture. Compared to
     traditional blocking I/O, which leaves bus bandwidth underutilized (as the SSD-to-CPU transfer
     must complete entirely before the CPU-to-GPU transfer begins), this strategy maximizes the hard-
     ware throughput of the entire I/O pipeline, significantly reducing the end-to-end model initialization
     latency.
        (2)I/O Transfer Optimization via Page-Locked Memory
        We utilized the page-locked memory mechanism—specifically, the .pin_memory() method for
     PyTorch tensors—to optimize the I/O transfer paths for input data such as .h5 files and initial frame
     images, thereby reducing the overhead of redundant host-to-host memory copies. The efficacy of
     this optimization lies in its ability to eliminate implicit data copying.
        In conventional heterogeneous transfer architectures relying on pageable memory, the GPU can-
     not safely execute Direct Memory Access (DMA) due to the dynamic paging of physical mem-
     ory addresses. Consequently, the CUDA driver must implicitly allocate a temporary pinned stag-
     ing buffer on the host. The CPU first copies the data from the pageable region to this staging
     buffer, after which the GPU’s DMA engine pulls the data across the PCIe bus into the VRAM.
     This ”double-copy” mechanism substantially exacerbates CPU scheduling overhead, host memory
     bandwidth consumption, and overall transfer latency.
        By adopting the pinned memory optimization paradigm, the physical addresses of the target
     memory pages are strictly locked into residency by the operating system. This allows the system
     to completely bypass the intermediate staging phase. The CUDA driver directly authorizes the
     GPU’s DMA controller to access and read the host’s physical memory via the PCIe bus, effectively
     eliminating the auxiliary CPU-to-CPU memory copy. However, it should be noted that because
     the inherent data volume of these specific input files is relatively small, the tangible performance
     gains derived from this particular I/O optimization remain marginal.
        (3)Asynchronous Non-Blocking Optimization for Video Frame Storage
        Subsequently, we implemented a non-blocking, asynchronous optimization pipeline for the
     entire lifecycle of video sequence frames, from generation to storage. This necessitated refactoring
     the save_results function within the unifolm-world-model-action/scripts/evaluation/world_model_interaction.py
     script, replacing it entirely with our custom save_results_async function.

     6.2.4. DeepCache Implementation.
       (1)Core Algorithmic Principles of DeepCache
       The denoising process in diffusion models unfolds iteratively, wherein spatial features exhibit
     merely marginal variations between adjacent timesteps. DeepCache capitalizes on this inherent
                                                                                                     34

temporal redundancy to precisely alleviate the massive computational burden of the U-Net ar-
chitecture. Within the network, the deep layers—tasked with constructing the global structural
contours—entail the most intensive and time-consuming matrix operations, yet their feature rep-
resentations evolve extremely slowly. Conversely, the shallow layers—responsible for generating
fine-grained textures—update more rapidly but incur minimal computational overhead.
   Consequently, DeepCache abandons the conventional paradigm of executing the complete U-Net
forward pass at every timestep. Instead, it bifurcates the denoising trajectory into alternating“Full
Step” and “Cached Steps”. During a Full Step, the model executes the entire network and caches the
computationally expensive deep features. In the subsequent Cached Steps, the algorithm physically
bypasses the dense and time-consuming matrix multiplications within the deep layers. It directly
retrieves the cached deep representations and exclusively executes the lightweight shallow layers
to refresh high-frequency details.
   In essence, the profound acceleration achieved by DeepCache stems from its ability to flawlessly
substitute highly repetitive and computationally prohibitive deep matrix operations with efficient
cache reads. This mechanism successfully offloads the most severe processing burden from the
GPU, realizing significant end-to-end speedups.
   (2)Analysis of the UnifoLM-WMA Denoising Process and U-Net Architecture
   During the video generation denoising process, the model must seamlessly integrate multimodal
conditions, spanning images, actions, and text. To underpin this complex joint spatiotemporal
denoising task, UnifoLM-WMA employs a highly customized, perfectly symmetric 3D U-Net ar-
chitecture.
   The entire network manifests a classic ”U-shaped” topology, wherein the forward propagation
of data is delineated into three distinct, functionally specialized stages:
 1) Input Stage (Feature Downsampling and Extraction): Comprising 13 network tiers, this
     phase executes progressive downsampling on the massive input volume. It systematically com-
     presses high-resolution surface pixel information into low-resolution, high-dimensional core
     abstract features.
 2) Bottleneck Stage (Deep Interaction and Fusion): Situated at the deepest stratum of the U-
     Net. Here, spatial dimensions are maximally compressed, yet computational density and la-
     tency reach their global peak. It shoulders the most computationally intensive spatiotemporal
     attention matrix operations, orchestrating the deepest semantic interaction across all modalities.
 3) Output Stage (Feature Restoration and Reconstruction): Symmetrically composed of 13
     tiers, this phase progressively upsamples and amplifies the deep abstract representations. By
     adeptly leveraging Skip Connections, it reintroduces high-frequency details from the Input
     Stage to ultimately reconstruct high-fidelity, pristine video frames.
   (3)DeepCache Optimization Strategy
   To overcome the severe PSNR degradation caused by error accumulation inherent in the origi-
nal static DeepCache mechanism, we deeply refactored the underlying architecture to introduce a
Timestep-Aware Dynamic Scheduling System.
   This system precisely regulates the operational mechanics of DeepCache through three mutually
decoupled control parameters:
 1) Intervention Timing: This parameter dictates when DeepCache is permitted to activate within
     the overall denoising trajectory. We enforce full forward passes (Full Steps) during the initial
     denoising phase to ensure the rigorous construction of the video’s underlying physical topology.
     The caching logic is exclusively initiated during the later fine-tuning stages, thereby maximally
     preserving the baseline PSNR.
                                                                                                      35

 2) Truncation Depth: This parameter determines the specific network layer at which the for-
    ward pass truncates and returns during a Cached Step. Retaining more shallow layers yields
    better preservation of mid-level semantic features, whereas an earlier truncation delivers a
    more aggressive reduction in computational overhead (FLOPs). This provides the system with
    architectural-level elasticity to navigate the Pareto Optimum between inference acceleration
    and image fidelity.
 3) Cache Frequency: This parameter controls how often a complete Full Step is forcefully in-
    jected during the DeepCache-enabled phase. By defining a rational interval cycle to serve as
    an “error calibratio” anchor for deep features, this strategy effectively mitigates the error accu-
    mulation stemming from prolonged reliance on outdated caches, striving to ensure the PSNR
    meets the required evaluation standards.

   The fidelity with which model-generated videos represent the physical world is fundamentally
governed by the underlying algorithmic architecture and the training corpus. However, it is also

   The DeepCache strategy exploits the temporal coherence of the U-Net architecture, specifically
the minimal variance in intermediate activations within its deeper layers during the denoising pro-

resulting in highly stable neural network feature maps. By caching and reusing the intermediate

degree of fidelity loss during video generation.
   In practice, however, we can qualitatively assess the variance dynamics of the deep intermediate
activations based on the model’s specific denoising trajectory. This enables the design of an adap-
tive and well-grounded DeepCache scheduling strategy to minimize the approximation-induced
degradation. The solution we proposed represents a relatively straightforward implementation of
this concept. Nevertheless, during the current tuning phase, we could not guarantee that the PSNR
threshold is met across all test cases. Future work will necessitate extensive empirical testing across
multiple parameter combinations to identify the optimal conservative parameter configuration, or
to develop a more sophisticated scheduling policy.

of parameter jitter during the generation process.

   In the practical implementation of model inference, the process consists of n iterations, as spec-
ified by the n_iter parameter in the run_world_model_interaction.sh script. Each iteration can be

   However, with respect to the attention computations involved in different parts of the denoising

data required for model inference is simultaneously loaded into both GPUs. One implementation
approach involves the CPU first reading the data from an SSD into memory, then transferring
it to the GPU local to the CPU, after which this GPU copies the data to the other GPU. Subse-
quently, both GPUs perform denoising in parallel, each handling their respective portion of the
attention computation. The computed attention results are then distributed across GPUs via block-
ing operations to facilitate subsequent steps, thereby enabling parallel processing of the attention
computation workload.
6.5. Conclusion.

mandated fidelity threshold (P SN R > 25dB), we adopted a balanced trade-off approach. Our
final deployment integrates mixed-precision, I/O optimizations, and FlashAttention; however, the
DeepCache strategy was excluded to preserve baseline quality, and operator fusion remains in
the exploratory phase. Under this configuration, the system achieved a mean acceleration ratio of
1.426×. Moving forward, we intend to further refine and synergistically combine diverse optimiza-
tion methodologies tailored to the specific algorithmic architecture, aiming to enhance inference
efficiency while ensuring full PSNR compliance.

                  7. Optimization for Numerical Relativity Code AMSS-NCKU
7.1. Introduction.
   General Relativity is the fundamental theory describing gravity. Although it has been established
for over a century, human understanding of it remains limited by the extreme complexity of the
Einstein field equations, which constitute one of the most challenging systems of partial differential
equations in physics. To analyze gravitational wave detection data, obtaining accurate numerical
solutions describing Binary Black Hole (BBH) mergers is crucial. Gravitational wave detection
was first realized in 2015 and awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2017. This breakthrough
was largely attributed to the numerical solutions of the Einstein field equations. Software used to
numerically solve these equations is collectively referred to as “numerical relativity codes”. AMSS-
NCKU is one of the few publicly available codes of this kind worldwide [4]. Its core technical
features include:
     • Numerical Methods: Adopting finite-difference methods to solve the equations.
     • Multi-scale Handling: Utilizing Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) technology to handle
       the inherent multi-scale characteristics of General Relativity.
     • Parallel Strategy: Adopting a Domain Decomposition strategy combined with MPI for
       parallel processing.
   In this competition, our main task is to perform numerical simulations of binary black hole merg-
ers and use technical means to minimize the total runtime of the simulation. It should be noted that
we integrated multiple software components (such as ccache) into a Docker container. These com-
ponents cache the compiled content, thereby shortening the time required for the compilation phase
in subsequent runs, even with partial modifications.
   By analyzing the workflow, reviewing the work logs, and conducting flame graph analysis on
the time-consuming steps of the project, we determined the direction for subsequent optimizations.
Through methods such as adjusting compiler parameters, changing compilation options, inlining
and vectorizing hotspot functions, grid partitioning, memory alignment, and asynchronous com-
munication, we reduced the total wall-clock time from 33.21 hours to 21.157 hours, achieving
a speedup of 1.539 ×.
   Meanwhile, through further exploration and analysis, we discovered potential optimization direc-
tions such as incomplete memory alignment optimization, dynamic load balancing, and the mixed
use of MPI and OpenMP. These will be the focus of our future work, with the aim of further short-
ening the runtime and achieving better optimization results.

7.2.2. Inline Function.
  The optimization of inline functions and computational kernels focuses on stripping away mem-
ory redundancy, function call overhead, and unnecessary diagnostic logic to streamline the critical
path:
    (1) Elimination of Ghost Copying: We removed the temporary array fh and the
        call symmetry_bd(...) invocation in diff_new.f90. This eliminates an O(N 3 ) mem-
        ory copy overhead. The kernel now directly accesses the original array f, handling bound-
        ary conditions via inlined logic.
    (2) Fast/Slow Path Strategy: To maintain performance without sacrificing safety, we split the
        computation into a Fast Path for the core region (pure calculation without branches) and a
        Slow Path for boundaries (with symmetry checks).
    (3) Loop Fusion: We aggressively fused the derivative calculations for the X, Y , and Z di-
        rections into a single, unified i loop. This structural transformation achieves a “load once,
        compute multiple times” paradigm. By consolidating these operations, we drastically re-
        duced redundant memory fetches, thereby maximizing both L1 Cache utilization and hard-
        ware register reuse rates.
    (4) Sanity Check Overhead Reduction: We identified a massive performance bottleneck at
        the entry of the RHS evaluation routines (e.g., bssn_rhs.f90,
        bssn_rhs_ss.f90, bssnE_Scalar_rhs.f90). The legacy code executed a global sum(...)
        operation across more than twenty 3D arrays for NaN sanity checking on every single call
        to compute_rhs_bssn. We wrapped this extremely expensive validation step within a
        preprocessor macro (#ifdef RHS_SANITY_CHECK), disabling it by default for production
        runs. This pragmatic adjustment eliminated a severe memory-scan hotspot without altering
        the mathematical results.

7.2.5. Non-blocking & Request Separation.
  To alleviate MPI communication bottlenecks and hide network latency, we overhauled the syn-
chronization routines in Parallel.C (transfer and transfermix) using advanced asynchronous
communication strategies and buffer caching:
    (1) Non-blocking Paradigm & Queue Separation: We transitioned from blocking commu-
        nications to a fully non-blocking paradigm. The request queues were explicitly split into
        independent recv_reqs and send_reqs. By systematically posting all MPI_Irecv calls
        prior to data packing and MPI_Isend invocations, we significantly reduced the MPI ren-
        dezvous waiting time.
    (2) Communication-Computation Overlap: To maximize the overlap between data trans-
        fer and data processing, we interleaved MPI_Testsome within the sending loop. This ac-
        tively polls the receive queue, triggering immediate UNPACK operations as soon as incoming
        chunks arrive. Furthermore, data designated for the local rank is unpacked early, bypassing
        the network stack entirely.
    (3) Buffer and List Caching: High-frequency communication steps were previously bottle-
        necked by dynamic memory allocation. We eliminated this overhead by implementing
        static caching for communication buffers (reusing MPI_Request, MPI_Status, and data
        arrays). Additionally, transfer lists for Sync operations are now cached, supported by
        a robust cache invalidation mechanism (InvalidateSyncCache) during adaptive mesh
        regrid events.
    (4) Final Synchronization: Overall structural correctness is guaranteed by a strict termination
        phase, utilizing MPI_Waitsome for pending receives and MPI_Waitall for the send queue,
        ensuring all ghost zones are fully updated before the next computational step.

7.4. Analysis and Optimization Plans.
  During AMR evolution, moving refined grids may cause workload imbalance across MPI pro-
cesses, which increases synchronization wait time and reduces parallel efficiency. To address this,
we plan to apply Dynamic Load Balancing (DLB): process workloads will be monitored periodi-
cally, and repartitioning will be triggered only when imbalance is significant.

activated only if I exceeds a predefined threshold, so migration overhead remains controlled.
   We plan to use a Hilbert space-filling curve (Hilbert SFC) for partitioning, as it improves both
load balance and spatial locality, thereby reducing communication overhead and improving long-
term AMR performance without modifying the physical model.

7.4.1. Hybrid MPI+OpenMP Parallelization Plan.
   In addition to dynamic load balancing, we plan to transition from a pure-MPI model to a hybrid
MPI+OpenMP model to improve node-level resource utilization on multi-core CPUs. The cen-
tral idea is to reduce the number of MPI ranks per node while introducing OpenMP threads for
intra-rank parallelism in compute-intensive loops (e.g., stencil updates and local reductions). This
strategy is expected to reduce communication pressure, alleviate synchronization overhead, and im-
prove memory locality through shared-memory execution within each rank. The implementation
will be incremental: first, identify thread-safe kernels and add OpenMP pragmas in hotspot regions;
second, tune rank-thread configurations and affinity policies; third, validate numerical consistency
against the baseline and measure scalability under both strong and weak scaling settings. The fi-
nal objective is to improve long-run throughput and parallel efficiency while preserving physical
correctness.

   In this project, we systematically optimized the AMSS-NCKU numerical relativity code to ac-
celerate high-fidelity Binary Black Hole (BBH) simulations. Through rigorous profiling, we iden-
tified critical bottlenecks: memory-bound 3D stencil operations, redundant instruction overhead,
and MPI communication latency.
   To address these, we applied a comprehensive, multi-tiered optimization strategy. At the com-
piler level, we leveraged the Intel toolchain with AVX2 vectorization, IPO, and strict 64-byte mem-
ory alignment. Algorithmically, we enhanced L1/L3 cache utilization via loop fusion, redundant
ghost copy removal, and cache blocking (tiling). Furthermore, we overhauled synchronization us-
ing fully asynchronous, non-blocking MPI to overlap computation and communication, while in-
tegrating ccache to streamline compilation. Together, these enhancements reduced the estimated
wall-clock time from 33.21 to 21.157 hours, delivering a projected speedup of 1.539× while pre-
serving strict physical and numerical correctness.
   Moving forward, our collaborative efforts will focus on unlocking deeper architectural perfor-
mance. Our primary objectives include implementing dynamic load balancing, enforcing com-
prehensive code-level memory alignment (e.g., via _mm_malloc and Fortran directives), and tran-
sitioning to a hybrid MPI+OpenMP parallelization model. These continuous improvements will
further maximize hardware utilization and push the scaling boundaries of large-scale simulations.
