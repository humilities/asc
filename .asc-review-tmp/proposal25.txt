1.1. The HPC Platform of XTU.
   Xiangtan University’s Super Heterogeneous Parallel Computing Cluster is a key project sup-
ported by the provincial finance under the “14th Five-Year Plan” for new infrastructure construc-
tion. The cluster includes ClusterEngine high-performance computing platform and AIstation arti-
ficial intelligence platform, and is open to all teachers and students of the university. The computing
capacity of the cluster is the theoretical computing capacity of 509 trillion times of CPU double-
precision peak performance (6,112 physical cores) and 1552 trillion times of GPU single-precision
theoretical computing capacity. The overall logical framework design diagram of the cluster is
shown in the Figure 1.




                  Figure 1. The structure and network of HPC platform at XTU

  The ClusterEngine high-performance computing platform consists of 1 management node, 1
login node, 1 physical monitoring platform, 85 general-purpose computing nodes, 2 large-memory

computing nodes, totaling 5472 CPU cores and a total memory capacity of 44.5TB. The nodes
are interconnected through Gigabit Ethernet and Infiniband. The management and login nodes are
each equipped with 16 CPU cores and 64GB of memory.
   The cluster has two resource partitions: CU and FAT partitions. The CU partition is configured
with 80 general-purpose computing nodes, each with 64 Intel 8358 CPU cores and 512GB of mem-
ory, totaling 5,120 CPU cores. The FAT partition is configured with 2 large-memory computing
nodes, each with 16 Intel 6334 CPU cores and 1TB of memory.
   The main function of the platform is to provide high-performance scientific and engineering com-
puting services to the entire university, meeting the needs of various disciplines for large-scale data
processing and large-scale scientific computing. The platform’s main application areas include pro-
viding high-performance computing environments for mathematics, statistics, mechanics, physics,
chemistry, chemical engineering, biology, earth science, aerospace science and technology, com-
puter science, and many other disciplines. The platform supports a wide range of application soft-
ware, such as MATLAB, OpenFOAM, ABINIT, GROMACS, NAMD and so on.
   The AIstation artificial intelligence platform consists of 10 GPU computing nodes, with a total
of 640 CPU cores, 80 NVIDIA A100 acceleration cards, a total memory capacity of 5TB, and
shared with the parallel storage and high-performance computing platform. The total capacity
is 1.3PB. The GPU’s measured peak speed can reach 888.9 trillion operations per second. This
platform supports single-machine and distributed running of mainstream AI frameworks as well as
task visualization.

   For example, the JXPAMG solver jointly developed with the Beijing Institute of Applied Physics

1.5. Key Achievements.
   As mentioned above, XTU’s research in supercomputing includes many aspects, and have pub-
lished multiple papers in international journals and conferences in the fields of mathematics and
computer science and so on. Such as CCF Transactions on High Performance Computing, Appli-
cable Analysis, Mathematical Methods in the Applied Sciences, International Conference on Man-
agement of Data(SIGMOD) International Conference on Very Large Data Bases(VLDB), IEEE
Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems(TPDS) and other journal conferences, here is a
brief overview of two of the outcomes:
   Innovation 1: Parallel Reservoir Simulators on Tianhe-2 [3]. Parallel reservoir simulators
are now widely used with availability of super computers. Modern massively parallel supercom-
puters demonstrate great power for simulating large-scale reservoir models. However, improving
scalability and efficiency for fully implicit methods on emerging parallel architectures is still chal-
lenging. In this work, we present a robust discretization together with a parallel linear solver algo-
rithm; and we explore the parallel implementation on the world’s fastest supercomputer Tianhe-2.
Starting with a general compositional model, we focus on the black oil model and developed Par-
allel eXtension Framework for parallelizing the serial simulator. A parallel preconditioner based
on fast auxiliary space preconditioning (FASP) is applied to solve the Jacobian system arising from
the fully implicit discretization. The parallel simulator was validated using large-scale black oil
benchmark problems, for which parallel scalabilities were tested. Giant reservoir models with over
100 million grid blocks have been simulated within a few minutes, and test the strong scalability
of AMG solver with 1 billion unknown. We also demonstrate the parallelization and acceleration
using Intel Xeon Phi coprocessors. In the end, the efficiency of the parallel simulator is illustrated
by a giant reservoir using up to 10,000 cores, for which the CPU and communication time are
summarized for the linear and nonlinear algorithms.
                                                                                                  6

   Innovation 2: Efficient Algorithms for the Maxwell Eigenvalue Problem [4]. The Maxwell
eigenvalue problem is a basic and important computational model in computational electromag-
netism, e.g., in regard to electromagnetic waveguides and resonances in cavities. This work pro-
posed two two-grid methods for the Maxwell eigenvalue problem. The new two-grid schemes re-
duce the solution of the Maxwell eigenvalue problem on a fine grid to one linear indefinite Maxwell
equation on the same fine grid and an original eigenvalue problem on a much coarser grid. The
new schemes, therefore, save total computational cost. The error estimates reveal that the two-
grid methods maintain asymptotically optimal accuracy, and the numerical experiments presented
confirm the theoretical results.

4.1. Analysis of HPL.
   The High-Performance Linpack (HPL) Benchmark serves as a standardized computational tool
for evaluating the floating-point performance capabilities of high-performance computing (HPC)
systems. By solving large-scale systems of linear equations Ax=b, i.e., Eq.4.1, where matrix A
represents an n × n large-scale dense matrix, this benchmark quantifies system performance in
floating-point operations per second (FLOPS). This method is one of the internationally recognized
standards for assessing supercomputer performance.
   One way to solve Eq.4.1 is Gaussian elimination, which first results in a triangular matrix by
first converting the A in the Ax=b equation into a triangular matrix by elimination.
   Suppose the solution problem is:

                              a11 a12 . . . a1n        x1          b1
                             a21 a22 . . . a2n   x2   b2 
                                                                 
                             ... ... ... ···  ···  =  ...                              (4.1)

5.1. Analysis of HPCG.
   The High Performance Conjugate Gradient (HPCG) benchmark is a key tool for assessing the
performance of high-performance computing (HPC) systems, particularly in simulating real-world
computational workloads. Unlike traditional benchmarks such as HPL, which mainly measure
floating-point operations through dense matrix multiplication, HPCG focuses on solving large
sparse linear systems. These systems are derived from the discretization of a three-dimensional par-
tial differential equation (PDE) that simulates a heat diffusion problem with zero Dirichlet boundary
conditions.
   At the heart of HPCG is the preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method, which neces-
sitates efficient handling of sparse matrix operations, optimized memory bandwidth, and parallel
computation across distributed systems. The global problem is defined on a three-dimensional grid
with dimensions (nx × npx) × (ny × npy) × (nz × npz), where nx, ny, and nz represent the local
subgrid sizes in the x, y, and z dimensions, respectively, assigned to each MPI process. The values
npx, npy, and npz denote the number of processes in each corresponding dimension. These values
are automatically computed during the HPCG setup phase but can also be manually specified in
the input file hpcg.dat or provided as command-line arguments.
   The HPCG benchmark consists of three critical computational kernels, each integral to evaluating
modern supercomputer architectures:
     (1) Sparse Matrix-Vector Multiplication (SpMV): The core computational task of HPCG, SpMV
         is computationally intensive and central to the benchmark’s performance evaluation.
     (2) Symmetric Gauss-Seidel (SymGS) Relaxation: This serves as the essential precondition-
         ing technique for the conjugate gradient method, enhancing the efficiency of the iterative
         solution process.
     (3) Conjugate Gradient Method: The iterative approach used to solve the large-scale sparse
         linear systems, making it a crucial component of HPCG’s overall performance.
   These kernels are commonly found in scientific computing applications, such as computational
fluid dynamics and electromagnetic simulations. They present significant challenges for system
memory bandwidth and fine-grained parallel efficiency, making them indispensable for bench-
marking modern HPC architectures.

5.2.3. Parallelization Paradigms for HPCG.
   In this section, we explore three different configurations for testing the standard HPCG bench-
mark: using MPI alone, using OpenMP alone, and using both MPI and OpenMP together. These
configurations allow us to compare the performance of the standard HPCG benchmark under dif-
ferent parallelization paradigms and understand the impact of each parallelization method.
   The matrix size used in our experiments is set to (160 × 160 × 128), with the runtime fixed at
1800 seconds. The third-party libraries used are OpenMPI and OpenBLAS, and the GNU Compiler
is employed for compilation.
   (1) HPCG with MPI
          When running the standard HPCG benchmark with MPI alone, the computation is paral-
       lelized across multiple processes, with each process handling a portion of the matrix. MPI
       handles the communication between processes, ensuring that data is exchanged correctly
       between different parts of the grid. This configuration is ideal for distributed memory sys-
       tems, where each node or process has its own memory and communicates over a network.
          The MPI-only version of HPCG can efficiently utilize multiple processors across a clus-
       ter, but the communication overhead between processes may limit performance as the num-
       ber of processes increases.
   (2) HPCG with OpenMP
          In contrast, using OpenMP alone focuses on parallelizing the computation within a sin-
       gle node, where multiple threads are used to take advantage of multi-core CPUs. OpenMP
       uses shared memory within a node, allowing threads to access common data structures with-
       out the need for explicit communication between them. This configuration is particularly
                                                                                                  24

Benchmark




       suitable for systems with multiple cores on a single node and can effectively speed up the
       computation by exploiting thread-level parallelism.
          While OpenMP is highly effective on multi-core processors, its performance may be
       limited by the memory bandwidth and CPU core utilization, especially when dealing with
       large-scale problems.
   (3) HPCG with MPI and OpenMP (Hybrid)
          A hybrid approach that combines MPI and OpenMP allows the standard HPCG bench-
       mark to take full advantage of both distributed memory and shared memory parallelism.
       In this configuration, MPI is used for inter-process communication across nodes, while
       OpenMP is used to parallelize computation within each node. This approach provides bet-
       ter scalability on multi-node systems, as it can efficiently utilize both multiple nodes (via
       MPI) and multiple cores within each node (via OpenMP).
          The hybrid MPI+OpenMP configuration is ideal for modern HPC systems, which often
       have many multi-core nodes connected via high-performance networks. By combining
       both parallelization models, this approach can deliver the best performance, provided that
       the problem size is sufficiently large and the load balancing between MPI and OpenMP is
       optimized.
   (4) Performance Comparison and Analysis
          Based on the results of the experiments, as shown in Figure 13, we observed that the con-
       figuration using MPI alone achieved the best performance across all tested machines. This
       configuration allowed the standard HPCG benchmark to fully utilize the distributed mem-
       ory capabilities of the system, with each MPI process handling a portion of the problem.
       The communication overhead, though present, was minimized due to the high efficiency of
       MPI in handling large-scale parallel problems.

       limited scalability on a single-node setup. Although OpenMP effectively leveraged multi-

          The hybrid MPI+OpenMP configuration performed well on multi-node systems, but

       hybrid configurations often require fine-tuning to achieve maximum performance, espe-

5.2.4. Hybrid Version of HPCG.
   To further optimize performance, a hybrid approach can be applied to the HPCG benchmark,
integrating CUDA for GPU acceleration. This allows the GPU’s floating-point performance to be
exploited without altering the basic conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm or preconditioner algorithm.
The GPU’s parallel processing power is harnessed to significantly speed up matrix operations,
which are a critical part of the HPCG benchmark.
   In this hybrid version, the matrix is analyzed, reordered, and the computational workload is
split between the CPU and GPU. The CPU handles portions of the matrix that are better suited for
traditional processors, while the GPU is responsible for the portions that benefit most from parallel
computation capabilities. This approach allows the hybrid version to capitalize on the strengths of
both the CPU and GPU, optimizing performance for large-scale computations.
   Fine-tuning the ratio of CPU to GPU workload is essential to achieve optimal performance. By
experimenting with different configurations and workloads, the ideal distribution of tasks between
CPU and GPU can be found, maximizing the overall performance of the HPCG benchmark on
systems equipped with both CPU and GPU resources.
   The hybrid version allows HPCG to fully utilize both CPU and GPU resources, providing an
effective solution for high-performance computing systems with multi-core CPUs and GPUs.

Mind, represents a significant advancement over AlphaFold 2 [5]. It is not only capable of pre-

6.3.1. Introduction to data parallelism.
   JAX Sharding Techniques
   Single Program Multiple Data (SPMD) in JAX is a parallelization technique where the same
computation (e.g., the forward pass of a neural network) can be executed in parallel across different
devices (such as multiple GPUs) with different input data (e.g., different inputs within a batch).
   JAX provides tools such as pjit, xmap, and PartitionSpec to enable efficient tensor sharding
across devices. In JAX, each array is associated with a Sharding object that specifies how the
tensor is distributed across devices. This mechanism allows for fine-grained control over how
computations are partitioned and executed, optimizing both memory usage and computational ef-
ficiency. By leveraging these tools, we can achieve scalable and high-performance execution of
AlphaFold 3’s inference process on multi-GPU setups.
   Data Parallelism
   Data Parallelism (DP) is a distributed computing technique that splits input data across multiple
devices (e.g., GPUs) for parallel processing. Each device independently computes the same model
but processes different subsets of the input data. The results are aggregated at the end of each
forward pass or layer computation. The overall architecture of data parallelism is illustrated in
Figure 16.
   A typical data parallel process can be described as follows:
    (1) Computational GPUs and Gradient Aggregation GPU: As shown in Figure 16, there are
        several computational GPUs (e.g., GPU0 to GPU2) and one GPU responsible for gradient
        aggregation, where the AllReduce operation is performed.
    (2) Model Replication: A complete copy of the model parameters is loaded onto each com-
        putational GPU.
    (3) Data Partitioning: The input data X (e.g., a batch) is evenly distributed among the com-
        putational GPUs. Each GPU performs a forward pass (FWD) and backward pass (BWD)
        on its subset of data, calculating a partial gradient G.
    (4) Gradient Push and Aggregation: Each computational GPU pushes its computed gradients
        to the gradient aggregation GPU, where the gradients are combined through an aggregation
        operation, typically gradient accumulation.
    (5) Gradient Pull and Model Update: After the aggregation is complete, the computational
        GPUs pull the aggregated gradients from the gradient aggregation GPU to update their
        local model parameters W . Once the update is complete, the model parameters on all
        computational GPUs remain consistent.
                                                                                               32




                      Figure 16. Overall Architecture of Data Parallelism

   (6) AllReduce Operation: The process of aggregating gradients and distributing the results
       back to the computational GPUs is referred to as the AllReduce operation. This ensures
       synchronization of model parameters across all devices while maintaining high computa-
       tional efficiency.

opt_einsum utilizes this optimal strategy for expressions involving up to four tensors. However,
even for larger networks comprising 9–10 tensors, the algorithm can compute the optimal path
within seconds, making it a practical choice for many applications.

            7. RNA m5 C Modification Site Detection and Performance Optimization




7.1. Introduction.
   RNA 5-methylcytosine (RNA m5 C) is a crucial epigenetic modification in which a methyl group
is added to the 5-carbon of cytosine, regulating gene expression by affecting RNA stability, splic-
ing, transport, and translation [14]. Unlike DNA methylation, RNA m5 C predominantly occurs in
mRNA, non-coding RNAs, and small RNAs [15]. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) technolo-
gies, such as ultrafast bisulfite sequencing(UBS-seq) [16] and RNA-Bis-seq [17], are used to detect
and analyze RNA m5 C by enabling the parallel sequencing of millions to billions of nucleotides,
thus advancing research in genomics, transcriptomics, and epigenetics.
   In this competition, our main task involves developing a workflow that systematically integrates
a specialized bioinformatics pipeline for detecting RNA m5 C in HTS data. The core focus of
this task is on the accuracy of m5 C site identification, false positive control, and computational
efficiency.
   Notably, we integrate several software components (e.g., hisat-3n, samtools, cutseq, and umicol-
lapse) into a single docker. Starting from raw sequencing data (FASTQ files), the pipeline performs
adapter trimming, low-quality sequence removal, rRNA/tRNA filtering, genome alignment, m5 C
site detection, and stringent filtering. The final output is a high-confidence list of m5 C candidate
sites with high accuracy and low false positives.
   By analyzing the overall workflow, dissecting individual commands, and conducting flame
graph analysis on the time-consuming steps, we have identified further opportunities for optimiza-
tion. Through adjusting parameters(Section 7.3.1), adopting file-based intermediary stor-
age(Section 7.3.2), parallelizing the workflow and applying parallelization at the hardware
level(Section 7.3.3), we successfully met the accuracy requirements while significantly reduc-
ing the overall workflow runtime. Specifically, the runtime was shortened from 6.9 hours to
1.33 hours, achieving a speedup of 5.188 times.
   Meanwhile, through further analysis and reflection, we have identified additional opportunities
for optimization, which will become a key focus of our future exploration. Specifically, we will
explore the potential of optimizing the compiler and compilation process, binding CPU cores, and
parallelizing key code segments to enhance the overall performance of the workflow.


7.2. Related Work.
   Parallelization is a critical approach to improving performance efficiency. By decomposing com-
putational tasks into multiple independent sub-tasks, it enables concurrent execution across mul-
tiple processing units or computing nodes. This drastically reduces computation time, especially
for large-scale data processing or complex computational tasks. Parallelization enhances hardware
resource utilization and accelerates computation-intensive tasks, making it a cornerstone in fields
such as HPC, deep learning, and big data analytics. Below are some commonly used parallelization
methods.
   (1) Task Parallelism
       Task parallelism involves dividing a large workflow into independent tasks that can be pro-
       cessed concurrently. This method is widely used in high-performance computing (HPC) to
                                                                                                  53

       maximize resource utilization across multiple processors. For example, Amdahl [18] pro-
       posed a model for understanding the limitations of parallel computing, while Chiu [19]pro-
       posed a novel task-parallel pipeline programming framework called Pipeflow. By decou-
       pling data abstractions from task scheduling, Pipeflow enables a more efficient implemen-
       tation of task-parallel pipeline algorithms compared to existing frameworks. .
   (2) Data Parallelism
       Data parallelism focuses on splitting large datasets into smaller chunks and processing each
       part independently. This is particularly useful in applications like genomics, simulations,
       and image processing. For instance, Patterson and Hennessy [20] systematically evalu-
       ated the performance of data parallelism across different computational architectures using
       quantitative analysis methods. Their work provides crucial insights into task partitioning
       in genomics and high-performance computing. Culler and Singh [21] introduced parallel
       computer architecture principles that enhance data parallelism.
   (3) Multi-threading
       Multi-threading allows multiple threads to run concurrently within a single process. This
       is effective for tasks that can be divided into smaller, concurrent operations. For example,
       Tullsen et al. [22] explored simultaneous multithreading, a technique that allows multiple
       independent threads to issue instructions to multiple functional units in a superscalar pro-
       cessor during a single cycle. Lo et al. [23] proposed Simultaneous Multithreading (SMT),
       a technique that allows multiple threads to share all processor resources during each cycle,
       enabling the alternation between thread-level parallelism and instruction-level parallelism.
   (4) Vectorization
       Vectorization allows multiple data points to be processed simultaneously through hard-
       ware capabilities such as Single Instruction, Multiple Data(SIMD) [24]. This method is
       commonly used in high-performance computing for numerical simulations. For example,
       Markus et al. present pipeline vectorization for synthesizing hardware pipelines based on
       software vectorizing compilers and improved efficiency and ease of development of hard-
       ware designs [25].

with different functions, in order to enhance the overall processing efficiency of the computing
system and significantly reduce the total execution time. Especially in large-scale computational
tasks, process parallelism can effectively distribute the computational load, alleviating the time
bottleneck caused by serial execution, thereby improving both the execution speed and resource
utilization of the task.

7.5. Optimization Plans.
    (1) Change the compile and compile options
           Optimizing compiler options and processes is a crucial approach to enhancing program
        performance and efficiency. By properly configuring compiler optimization flags, ad-
        justing linking options, parallelizing the compilation process, and performing hardware-
        specific optimizations, we can significantly improve execution speed, memory utilization,
        and computational capacity. We have already tried using different compilers (such as the
        AMD compiler that matches the server) and various compiler optimization flags. How-
        ever, these optimizations did not yield the expected results. Therefore, we plan to explore
        additional optimization strategies. Below are some potential avenues for improvement:
         (a) Compiler Optimization Flags Compiler optimization flags allow various optimiza-
             tions during the compilation process to improve program performance. These flags
             control how the code is generated, and can reduce memory consumption and improve
             execution efficiency. In addition to the commonly used flags such as -O2, -O3, and
             -funroll-loops, the following options can also be considered:
                 • -march=native: Optimizes the code based on the hardware features of the cur-
                   rent machine, allowing the program to fully leverage the CPU instruction set
                   (e.g., AVX, SSE).
                 • -ftree-vectorize: Automatically vectorizes the code, utilizing SIMD (Single
                   Instruction, Multiple Data) instructions to accelerate computations on supported
                   processors.
         (b) Link-time Optimization (LTO) Link-time optimization (LTO) performs cross-file op-
             timizations during the linking phase, allowing the compiler to optimize across modules,
             further improving performance.
                                                                                         63

              • -flto: This option enables LTO, optimizing unused code, eliminating redundant
                functions, and enhancing overall performance through cross-module optimiza-
                tion.
     (c) Profile-Guided Optimization (PGO) Profile-guided optimization (PGO) optimizes
         the most frequently used parts of the program by collecting runtime performance data.
         It typically involves two steps:
              • First, compile the program with -fprofile-generate to gather performance
                data.
              • Second, recompile the program with -fprofile-use based on the collected data
                to optimize performance bottlenecks.
     (d) Optimized Math and Threading Libraries For compute-intensive applications, us-
         ing optimized math libraries and threading libraries can significantly boost program
         performance:
              • Use optimized math libraries such as Intel MKL or OpenBLAS to accelerate ma-
                trix operations and other numerical computations.
              • Use threading libraries like OpenMP or Intel Threading Building Blocks
                (TBB) for efficient parallel computing support.
(2) Binding CPU cores
       When multiple tasks are executed in parallel, occupying the entire CPU, binding CPU
    cores (i.e., assigning specific processes or threads to fixed CPU cores) offers several ad-
    vantages:
     (a) Reduced Cache Misses: By binding tasks to specific cores, data can remain resident
         in the cache of that core, which reduces the frequency of cache misses and enhances
         data access speed.
     (b) Minimized Context-Switching Overhead: Binding tasks to designated cores reduces
         the frequency of task migrations between cores, thus minimizing the performance
         penalties associated with context switching.
     (c) Reduced Resource Contention: By allocating tasks to different cores, competition
         for shared resources (such as L3 cache and memory bandwidth) between cores is min-
         imized, improving overall system performance.
     (d) Improved Real-Time Performance: Binding critical tasks to specific cores ensures
         they are not disturbed by other tasks, thus fulfilling real-time performance require-
         ments.
       In conclusion, this strategy may potentially enhance the execution efficiency of certain
    tasks.
(3) Parallel processing in hisat_3n_table.cpp
       We performed a flame graph analysis on one of the time-consuming steps, the hisat-3n-table
    step, as shown in Figure 40, and identified that the performance bottleneck primarily lies
    in the locking mechanisms within the producer-consumer model. To address this issue, we
    plan to optimize the producer part of the code. Specifically, we will conduct preliminary
    processing and filtering of each line in the input file and place the processed data into a
    thread pool for further analysis by the consumer. To accelerate the producer’s processing
    speed, we intend to process each line of data in the input file in parallel based on chromo-
    somes, thereby reducing competition and wait times for the locking mechanisms, which
                                                                                                 64

       will increase the throughput of the producer and optimize overall performance. Theoreti-
       cally, by implementing these optimizations, we expect to improve the program’s execution
       efficiency and shorten its runtime.
